A speed test that doubles as a hardware diagnostic

Every other page here measures you. This one can also measure your mouse, because the most common failure mode in a mechanical mouse switch shows up as timing. As the metal contacts inside a switch wear, they start to bounce on contact, and the switch reports two presses where your finger made one. The two reports arrive absurdly close together — usually well under a twentieth of a second — because no human intends to click twice that fast.

So this page plots the gap between every consecutive pair of clicks in your run as a histogram, in forty-millisecond buckets, and counts separately how many of those gaps fell under eighty milliseconds. A healthy mouse driven by a human puts its weight in the middle of the chart. A failing switch drops a distinct spike into the very first bucket, which is drawn in red so it is hard to miss.

Read it with a little care. If you were deliberately double-clicking as fast as you possibly could, some short gaps are yours and not the switch's. The unambiguous version of the test is to click at a normal, deliberate pace — roughly two or three times a second, taking your time — for the full run. Any sub-eighty-millisecond gap that appears while you are clicking slowly did not come from your finger.

Questions

How do I know if my mouse is double-clicking on its own?

Run this test clicking slowly and deliberately, two or three times a second, and watch the first histogram bucket. Clicking that slowly, you cannot physically produce a gap under eighty milliseconds — so if the counter above the chart is not zero, those extra clicks came from the switch.

What causes double-click drift?

Contact wear inside the switch. The internal metal leaf loses tension over millions of actuations and starts bouncing when it closes, so one press produces two electrical contacts. Techniques that deliberately chatter the switch, drag clicking above all, bring it on much sooner.

Can a double-clicking mouse be fixed?

Sometimes. Some mice let you raise the debounce time in their software, which masks the fault at the cost of a little input latency; replacing the switch fixes it properly if you can solder. Otherwise it is a symptom of a mouse near the end of its service life.
