A minute is an endurance test with a click counter attached

Sixty seconds is long enough that the test stops being about your hand and starts being about your plan. Going out at your ten-second pace guarantees a collapse somewhere past the halfway mark; going out conservatively leaves speed on the table you can never get back. The best minute-long scores almost always come from people who picked a rate slightly below their peak and simply refused to drop it.

It is also the length that most clearly separates technique from effort. A grip that works by tensing the whole forearm cannot survive a minute — the tension is the thing that fails. A movement that comes from the finger, with the wrist resting, degrades far more gently, and that shows up as a much flatter stability figure under your result.

One practical warning: a minute of hard clicking is a genuine repetitive load on a small set of tendons, and it is the run most likely to leave you sore. If you feel anything sharp in your forearm or the back of your hand, stop. This is a browser toy, not a sport worth an injury.

Questions

How much slower should sixty seconds be than ten?

Ten to thirty percent slower is typical. If you lose more than about a third of your ten-second pace, the limiting factor is stamina rather than speed and a lighter, finger-led technique will help more than clicking harder.

Is a 60 second click test bad for my hand?

It can be if you repeat it. A single run is fine for most people; back-to-back minute runs put a real repetitive load on the flexor tendons in your forearm. Space them out and stop at the first sign of soreness.

What is a good 60 second CPS?

Four to six is a solid sustained rate. Seven or more held for a full minute is genuinely fast, and past that you are into trained-technique territory.
