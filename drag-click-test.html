Friction, not fingers

Drag clicking does not really involve clicking. You drag a finger down the face of the mouse button so that friction makes the button chatter, tripping the switch repeatedly on a single stroke. Rates in the tens of clicks per second are routine, which sounds impressive until you notice that none of them were intentional presses.

It depends almost entirely on hardware. The shell has to have enough grip — some mice are famous for it and others cannot do it at all — and the switch has to be the kind that will chatter rather than debounce the contact away. Skin, humidity and whether you have just washed your hands all change the result, which is why the same person can post fifteen CPS one minute and nothing at all the next.

This is why the number below your result is a count of bursts rather than a rate. The test looks for stretches where your clicking crossed fifteen clicks per second inside a one-second window and counts how many separate stretches there were. Treat it as an estimate and nothing more: drag clicking is inherently fuzzy to detect, and a figure like this is trivially gamed by anything that generates fast contacts. It is not a leaderboard-grade measurement and it is not offered as one.

Questions

Why is my drag click score zero?

Most likely your mouse cannot do it. Smooth or coated shells give the finger nothing to catch on, and switches with aggressive debouncing throw the extra contacts away before they reach the browser. A dry finger and a grippier surface are the two things worth trying before concluding anything.

Is drag clicking bad for my mouse?

Yes, unambiguously. It is the hardest thing you can do to a mechanical switch — you are deliberately producing the contact bounce that switch design exists to prevent, thousands of times a minute. Mice used for drag clicking develop genuine double-click faults far sooner than mice that are not.

Why is this only an estimate?

Because the browser sees contacts, not intent, and a burst of contacts looks the same whether friction produced it or something else did. Any figure a web page can put on drag clicking is approximate, and a page that presents one as an exact leaderboard score is overselling what it can actually know.
