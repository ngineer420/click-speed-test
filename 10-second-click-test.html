Why ten seconds became the standard

If someone quotes you a CPS number without saying how long they clicked for, they almost certainly mean ten seconds. The length came out of Minecraft PvP, where the Kohi server's in-game counter sampled ten seconds, and it stuck because it sits at a useful point on the fatigue curve: long enough that technique and control show up, short enough that almost anybody can hold their best pace for the whole run.

Ten seconds is where the difference between clicking fast and clicking well starts to appear. Over five seconds you can brute-force a good number. Over ten, a start that is too aggressive shows up as a visible sag in the last three seconds, and the stability figure below your result will say so.

This is also the length to use if you want to compare yourself to anything published. Leaderboards, forum posts and the numbers in our guide to what counts as a good CPS are all ten-second figures unless they say otherwise.

Questions

What is a good 10 second CPS score?

Most people land between four and seven. Above eight is fast for a normal grip, and ten or more over a full ten seconds generally means jitter or butterfly clicking.

Why did ten seconds become the default?

It came from Minecraft PvP servers whose in-game CPS counters sampled ten seconds, and every click-test site copied the convention. There is nothing magic about the number beyond being the one everybody else uses.

Should I pace myself or go flat out?

Flat out, but from a pace you can actually hold. Starting above your sustainable rate and sagging usually produces a lower average than starting at the right rate and holding it — the stability readout under your result will show you which one you did.
